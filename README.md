# Genetic-Variations-In-Asia
An interactive group project exploring the gene pool among the population of Asia

Overview

This project examines genetic mutations, population diversity, and biological adaptations across four Asian countries: India, Pakistan, Indonesia, and South Korea. The study explores how genetic variation is influenced by population history, migration, geographic isolation, environmental pressures, and demographic structure.
The project was primarily inspired by the GenomeAsia 100K Project, an international genomic initiative designed to improve representation of Asian populations in global genomic research. Its 2019 pilot study analysed 1,739 genomes representing 219 population groups across Asia and highlighted substantial genetic variation that had previously been underrepresented or misclassified in global genomic databases.
Through comparative case studies, this project connects fundamental genetics with human evolution, medical genetics, precision medicine, pharmacogenomics, and population genomics.

Objectives

The project aimed to:

•	Understand the biological significance of genetic mutations.

•	Examine physical, chemical, biological, and environmental factors affecting DNA.

•	Explore genetic variation among selected Asian populations.

•	Investigate examples of genetic adaptation and disease-associated variants.

•	Understand how migration, isolation, and population history shape genetic diversity.

•	Examine the importance of Asian genomic representation in modern medicine.

•	Explore applications of population genetics in precision healthcare and evolutionary research.

Conclusion

The genetic landscapes of India, Pakistan, Indonesia, and South Korea demonstrate the complexity of human genetic diversity across Asia.
Population history, migration, geographic isolation, environmental pressures, and demographic structure have contributed to distinct genetic patterns and adaptations. At the same time, substantial diversity exists within each country, making simplistic country-level genetic classifications inadequate.
The project highlights a broader scientific principle: genomic medicine requires genomic representation. Expanding Asian genomic datasets can improve understanding of disease-associated variants, drug response, human evolution, and population-specific biology.
